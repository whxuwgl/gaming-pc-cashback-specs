## What "gaming pc tower" actually covers

The phrase gets used for at least three different products, and the listings don't always make the difference obvious:

- **Prebuilt mid-tower desktops** — a case, motherboard, CPU, GPU, RAM, storage and PSU assembled and warrantied as one unit. This is what most people searching "gaming pc tower" end up buying.
- **Small form factor (SFF) builds** — same idea, cramped case. They look great on a desk and run hotter and louder, and upgrades are painful because the GPU clearance is measured in millimetres.
- **Barebones or "configure your own" systems** — a base configuration you upgrade at checkout. The base price is the marketing, the real price is whatever you add.

If you're comparing a tower against a gaming laptop, the honest trade is upgradeability versus portability. A tower lets you swap the GPU in two years and keep everything else. A laptop doesn't, and a desktop-class GPU in a thin chassis is still a compromise.

## The spec lines that decide whether the price is fair

Skip the big numbers on the product title and check these instead.

| Component | What to look for | Where cheap builds cut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GPU | The exact model, plus VRAM. "RTX 5060" and "RTX 5060 Ti 16GB" are very different purchases | Older-generation GPUs recycled into "new" listings |
| CPU | A gaming CPU that doesn't bottleneck the GPU. A 6-core chip is usually a better match than a discounted 20-core | High core counts paired with mid-range GPUs to look premium |
| RAM | 16GB minimum for 1080p, 32GB if you multitask while playing. Dual channel, and check the rated speed | Single stick in a 16GB build — measurably slower |
| PSU | Named brand, 80 Plus Bronze or better, wattage with headroom over the GPU's requirement | Unbranded units, or a wattage figure with no efficiency rating at all |
| Storage | 1TB NVMe, PCIe 4.0 at this point | 1TB SATA SSD, or a DRAM-less drive that slows badly once it fills |
| Case | Mesh front, at least two intake fans, GPU length clearance listed | Tempered glass front and one exhaust fan |

A tower with a strong GPU and a weak PSU isn't a bargain, it's a machine you'll rebuild in eighteen months. If the listing doesn't state the PSU model and the RAM configuration, that's itself the answer.

## Where the money quietly goes missing

The markup on a prebuilt isn't hidden — it's just spread across things you weren't comparing:

- **PSU and motherboard generics.** The components nobody photographs.
- **A Windows licence that's already priced in**, versus a build-your-own where you buy it separately. Worth checking which side of the line your quote sits on.
- **Extended warranties.** These are pure margin on desktops, which already carry a manufacturer warranty on the parts.
- **RGB.** Fans, strips and a controller can add three figures to a build without adding a single frame per second.
- **Overspecced CPUs.** If the GPU is the bottleneck, extra cores change nothing in games.

The flip side: a prebuilt arrives assembled, tested, and covered by one warranty instead of seven. For a first build, that's a legitimate thing to pay for. Just pay for it knowingly.

## Prebuilt or build it yourself?

Building is usually cheaper on comparable parts and you learn the machine. Prebuilt is faster, comes with a single warranty, and you're not troubleshooting a no-POST on a Tuesday night.

The calculation that actually matters: compare the prebuilt price against the same parts bought individually. If the gap is under roughly the cost of a Windows licence plus a couple of hours of your time, the prebuilt is the better call. If the gap is a few hundred dollars, the parts list wins.
